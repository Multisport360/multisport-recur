# Multi-Sport Recur — training plan research (draft for CSCS review)

**Status:** draft, written Nov 17, 2026. Not coach-reviewed. The templates in `data/plan_templates.draft.json` come from this research and must be reviewed and signed off by a certified strength and conditioning coach (CSCS) before any family sees them.

## How the plans work

1. The gap report finds the trainable stats with the biggest gap to the next level.
2. The app picks at most 2 **focus modules** for those stats (sprint speed, jumping power, throwing velocity and so on).
3. The focus modules are added to a shared **strength base**: 2 days a week in the Foundation tier, 3 in the Development tier.
4. Every plan is a 6-week block that ends with a retest.
5. Safety screens run first: current pain, throwing-arm pain, training experience and season phase. A fifth question asks how many team practices or games the athlete has in a typical week; each one counts as a hard day toward the weekly limit.

All of this is plain code choosing from fixed templates. The AI never writes or changes a plan.

## What the research supports

### Strength training is safe and effective for teens when supervised
- The 2014 International Consensus (endorsed by the AAP and others) supports supervised resistance training for youth and gives guidance on teaching and progressing it by age. [S1]
- Common youth guidance: 2–3 sessions a week on non-consecutive days, starting with 1–2 sets and progressing. [S2, S3]
- Long-term athletic development: build general athleticism and strength first, then specialise. [S4]
- **Draft choice:** two tiers based on training experience, not age alone. All 8th graders start in Foundation.

### Sprint speed (40, 60-yard dash, 100 m, 200 m)
- Plyometric training improved both jumping and sprinting in young athletes, across 33 studies and 1,499 players. [S8]
- Resisted sprints (sled or vest) improve sprint times in young athletes, but in one youth meta-analysis they were no better than plain sprinting. [S9] A 2025 meta-analysis found resisted sprints helped more for the first 10 m and in youth athletes. [S10]
- **Draft choice:** short full-effort sprints with full rest are the core. A light sled is optional and Development-only.
- Nordic hamstring curls: programs that include them cut hamstring injuries by about half. [S11] They're in Strength A for everyone who sprints.

### Jumping power (vertical, broad, long jump, high jump)
- Plyometrics improve vertical jump in young athletes. [S8]
- **Draft choice:** count foot contacts. Foundation stays around 40–60 a session; Development around 60–100. A CSCS should confirm these numbers.

### Throwing velocity (fastball, infield/outfield velocity, catcher pop time)
- Resistance training improves overhead throwing velocity. One meta-analysis pooled 16 studies and recommends at least 4 weeks at 2–3 sessions a week. [S12, S14]
- **Weighted balls are left out.** In a 6-week trial with high school pitchers, the weighted-ball group gained about 3% velocity, but 24% of them were injured, compared with none in the regular-ball group. [S7]
- Pitch Smart (MLB and USA Baseball) rules are built into the plan: pitch counts and rest days, no pitching three days in a row, at least 4 months a year off competitive pitching with 2–3 months in a row of no overhead throwing, and no catching on rest days. [S5]
- Coming back from a throwing break: build up over 4–6 weeks for ages 13–14, or 6–8 weeks for ages 15–18, throwing 3–4 days a week. [S6]

### Rotational power (exit velocity, shot put, discus)
- High school hitters who added rotational and full-body medicine ball work 3 days a week for 12 weeks were studied against hitters who only lifted. [S13]
- Overload/underload bat training has research behind it, but mostly in college players, so it's **left out** of the draft. The CSCS can add it to the Development tier.

### Speed endurance and distance running (400 m, hurdles, 800 m, 1600 m)
- The youth running consensus statement says there is still no evidence-based guidance on safe running distances for youth at different ages. Current advice is opinion-based. [S15]
- **Draft choice:** progression is by time rather than mileage, every bigger week is followed by an easier one, and there's at least one full rest day a week. These volumes need the closest CSCS review of anything in the draft.
- Hurdle and throws technique belongs to the athlete's event coach. The plan builds the athletic qualities behind it.

### Height and weight
- Height can't be trained, so the app says so.
- Weight: the app doesn't set weight or calorie targets for teens. It points families to the athlete's doctor or a registered dietitian. An AAP clinical report covers healthy weight practices in young athletes, including unhealthy ways athletes try to gain weight. [S16]

## Questions for the strength coach

1. Are the Foundation and Development tiers, and the "1 year supervised" cut-off, right?
2. Are the plyometric contact counts and sprint volumes appropriate for each tier?
3. Should a light sled stay in Development, or come out?
4. Are the distance-running volumes safe for 8th–12th graders?
5. Should overload/underload bats be added to the Development tier?
6. Are the in-season reductions enough when the athlete also has team practice? (The plan now counts each team practice or game as one hard day. Is one hard day per practice or game the right count?)
7. Which exercise swaps should the app offer when a family has no gym (bodyweight-only versions)?
8. Is anything missing that should be a hard stop (a reason to show no plan)?
